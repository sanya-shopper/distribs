2 · Where the randomness comes from

A pseudo-random generator (here xoshiro256++, Blackman & Vigna 2021) emits 64-bit integers; the top 53 bits become a uniform double U on [0,1). The master trick that turns U into anything else is inverse-transform sampling: push U through the inverse CDF, X = F−1(U). Watch it below: each uniform draw enters on the vertical axis, reflects off the CDF, and lands on the horizontal axis — where the curve is steep, the landings crowd together, and the histogram grows into the target density. The target F selector makes the generality vivid: the same one-uniform trick manufactures the exponential, the Rayleigh of the birthday bound, and the Gumbel of extreme values, just by swapping the curve.

Where they come from: the uniform is classical probability's silent axiom — Laplace's "equally possible cases" (1812) — reborn at Los Alamos in the 1940s when Ulam and von Neumann made uniform digits the fuel of the Monte Carlo method. The exponential became physically real when Rutherford and Soddy (c. 1900) found radioactive decay obeys it exactly; it is provably the only memoryless continuous law, the clock of every Poisson process, and the constant-hazard baseline of reliability and survival analysis.

In the field: under a true null hypothesis a p-value is itself Uniform(0,1) — the t-test playground below shows the flat histogram live. The sibling experiment shastats (paper §4.1) used that as an instrument: its 256 per-prefix goodness-of-fit p-values, tested against the flat line, came back uniform (Kolmogorov–Smirnov p = 0.62), and its scariest single reading — a prefix at p = 0.0015 — deflates once you recall the minimum of 256 uniforms is Beta(1, 256): Pr[min ≤ 0.0015] ≈ 0.32. An ordinary bottom of the class, not a finding.

2.2 · The generator under the microscope

xoshiro256++ (Blackman & Vigna 2021) is a scrambled linear generator: a linear engine advances the 256-bit state with only XORs, shifts and rotations — a linear map over the two-element field — and a small nonlinear scrambler, rotl(s₀+s₃, 23) + s₀, hashes each state into the output word. Linear engines are the one class mathematics can fully analyse; the scrambler erases their linear fingerprints without touching the state, so it provably cannot shorten the cycle.

Proved: the engine's characteristic polynomial — degree 256, weight 115, spelled out in paper §2.2 and rederived from the C code itself by scripts/charpoly.py, which certifies primitivity against the complete factorization of 2²⁵⁶−1 — is primitive, so the state walks a single cycle through every nonzero 256-bit state: period exactly 2²⁵⁶−1. The all-zero state is the unique fixed point and must never be used (why ps_rng_seed() feeds every seed through splitmix64). Jumping ahead is a matrix power, computable exactly: the published jump/long_jump functions advance precisely 2¹²⁸ and 2¹⁹² steps, giving parallel streams that are guaranteed never to overlap. For the sibling * / ** scramblers the authors prove 4-dimensional equidistribution at this state size. Measured: no systematic failures on TestU01's BigCrush battery (L'Ecuyer & Simard 2007), clean long PractRand runs, and a pass on the Hamming-weight dependency test the authors invented after finding that raw linear engines carry popcount biases BigCrush cannot see. Open — and out of scope: no theorem says any practical generator passes statistical tests (test-passing is evidence, not proof, and the equidistribution theorem is stated for the multiplicative scramblers, not ++); and xoshiro256++ is not cryptographic — a modest number of outputs lets an adversary reconstruct the state, so it must never guard secrets.

Below, the claim made visible: each row is the 64 bits of one output. On the left, xoshiro256++ (the same algorithm as src/rng.c, ported to JavaScript). On the right, the low-order bits of a classic power-of-two LCG — provably periodic with tiny period, which is why the stripes appear for every seed you try.

3 · The discrete family: counts

A yes/no event (Bernoulli), how many yeses in n tries (binomial — proportions, A/B tests, acceptance sampling), how long until the first yes (geometric — retries, attrition), and how many events occur when opportunities are many but each is rare (Poisson — arrivals, decays, claims, count regression). Drag the parameters and watch the shapes respond; hover any stem for its exact probability.

Binomial(n, p)

History: born in the 1654 Pascal–Fermat letters on splitting interrupted stakes; de Moivre (1733) found the bell curve hiding in it — the first CLT. Mendel's 3:1 pea ratios and Bell Labs' acceptance sampling are binomial counts.

In the field: the sibling experiment shastats (paper §3.2; a local repo, not yet online) used Binomial(256, ½) as the yardstick for SHA-256's avalanche: flip one input bit and the count of flipped output bits should have mean 128, spread 8. Over 419 million exhaustive single-bit-flip pairs: mean 128.0000, spread 8.0003. Each of the 6,400 (input bit, output bit) cells is an exact binomial coin — the worst one, +3.9σ, is priced under the Gumbel.

Poisson(λ)

History: derived by Poisson (1837) for jury verdicts, made famous by Bortkiewicz's horse-kick deaths in the Prussian cavalry (1898), then confirmed twice over: Erlang's telephone arrivals (1909) and Rutherford–Geiger's alpha-particle counts (1910).

Geometric(p)

History: "how many throws until the first six?" is as old as dice. The only memoryless discrete law — the gambler's fallacy is the refusal to believe it. Today: network retransmissions, cycles-to-conception, first-defect inspection.

In the field: a hash digest's count of leading zero bits — the quantity Bitcoin's proof of work thresholds — is tails before the first head: Geometric(½), Pr[N=k] = 2−(k+1). The sibling experiment shastats (paper §3.3) tried the law on 2³³ SHA-256 digests with no parameter fitted: fit p = 0.30, mean 1.000011 against the theoretical 1.

Bernoulli(p): the atom

Everything on this row is built from the single yes/no experiment: the binomial is n Bernoullis summed, the geometric counts failures before the first success, and the Poisson is the n→∞ limit of rare Bernoullis. In the library the sampler is one comparison: ps_runif(rng) < p.

History: named for Jacob Bernoulli, whose Ars Conjectandi (1713) proved the first law of large numbers — the "golden theorem" that frequencies converge to p — and made the coin toss the founding object of statistics.

Negative binomial(r, p)

History: a gambling wait in Montmort (1714), reborn with Greenwood & Yule (1920) as the law of accident proneness — the original overdispersion argument. Today it prices multi-collision searches against an ideal hash (see the collision statistics page of the sister site) and models clumped counts from insurance claims to RNA-seq.

The Bitcoin double-spend race — the two hats on one head

A merchant waits for z confirmations — z further blocks on the honest chain — before releasing the goods. An attacker holding a fraction q of the network's hash power mines a competing chain in secret, hoping to overtake it and undo the payment. Blocks are found by a Poisson process, so each new block is the attacker's with probability q and honest with probability p = 1−q, independently. The number of blocks the attacker finds while the honest chain advances by z is then exactly NB(z, p): attacker blocks are the failures before the z-th honest success. Take the other route and the other hat appears — the honest network needs a gamma (Erlang) amount of time to find z blocks, and given that time the attacker's count is Poisson, so the count is a gamma-mixed Poisson. Same law, both derivations.

The Bitcoin white paper (2008) takes a third route in its §11: it replaces that random elapsed time by its mean, and so arrives at a Poisson count with λ = zq/p. The mean is right; the dispersion is not. The negative binomial's variance is zq/p² = (1/p) × mean, so the Poisson understates it by that factor — 11% at q = 0.1, 67% at q = 0.4 — and understating the spread understates exactly the tail the merchant is buying protection against. Rosenfeld (2014) corrected it — declining the assumption and modelling the count “more accurately as a negative binomial variable”; Grunspan & Pérez-Marco (2018) give the exact risk in closed form, and it is one more incomplete-beta reduction: P(z) = I4pq(z, ½), the same ps_incbeta() that serves the t and F tables. The true risk shrinks by a factor 4pq for each further confirmation — 0.36 at q = 0.1 — while the Poisson version shrinks by 0.27, strictly faster. Two decay constants is the signature of two different models. Drag q and z and watch the gap open.

Beta-binomial(n, a, b) — first pick a coin, then flip it

Every distribution above flips one coin. The beta-binomial asks what happens when the coin itself is drawn at random — a proportion p from a Beta(a, b), once per unit — and only then flipped n times. Two experiments, easy to confuse:

One coin, many flips. Draw p once for the whole world, then flip it for every trial in every family. This is the binomial: families differ only by luck.
One coin per family. Each family draws its own p, then flips that coin n times. Luck still operates, but now families also differ in their propensity — and the counts spread out.

The means are identical. The variance is not: mixing multiplies it by 1 + (n−1)ρ, where ρ = 1/(a+b+1) is the intraclass correlation — the chance that two children of the same family share an outcome beyond what the common rate explains. ρ = 0 is the binomial; ρ = 1 is a coin that comes up all-heads or all-tails per family. Drag ρ and watch the blue stems (binomial) and magenta stems (beta-binomial) separate.

Four sons — is the next one more likely to be a son?
Could you even tell? Tarone's test on simulated sibships of four, drawn with the ρ set above. Press a button.

History: the mixture is as old as Bayes' 1763 essay — his billiard ball fixes an unknown p before any ball is thrown — and became a working tool with Skellam (1948), who named and fitted it. Its two natural homes are biology, where each litter, couple or plot has its own propensity, and manufacturing, where each production lot has its own defect rate.

In the field: the sibling experiment shastats (paper §5.4) ran this audit on 256 populations of SHA-256 digests: a real prefix dependence would be exactly the each-cluster-draws-its-own-p signature above, dispersion index > 1. Measured: 1.00 within noise. Its one flagged reading pointed the other way — one bin of the double hash SHA256d varied less across prefixes than chance allows (index 0.756, p = 0.003) — and was filed as a curiosity, not a finding: under-dispersion is a different model, and with 574 quantities examined, ~5.7 readings below p = 0.01 were expected (4 appeared).

4 · The continuous family: measurements

Explore: hover (or tap-drag) any curve below to read off the density and the cumulative probability P(X ≤ x) at your cursor — the area up to it shades live — and press draw 400 samples to watch a simulated histogram assemble under the theoretical curve. Samples come from the same algorithms as the C library; they reset whenever you move a parameter.

Normal — the polar Box–Muller sampler

The normal's inverse CDF has no closed form, so the library earns its normals by rejection: rain uniform points on the square, keep the ~79% that land in the disc, and reshape each survivor's radius into a perfect bell curve (Box & Muller 1958). Press play and watch the histogram on the right converge to N(0,1).

History: de Moivre found the curve inside the binomial (1733); Gauss tied it to least squares as the law of errors (1809, hence "Gaussian"); Laplace's CLT (1810) explained its universality; Quetelet fit it to people (1835), Maxwell to molecules (1860). The name "normal" spread via Galton and Pearson. Caution lives in the tails — real extremes, finance above all, are heavier than Gaussian.

Paper: §4.3 & Figure 6 — PDF · Code: src/dist.c: ps_normal() · Box & Muller (1958)

The central limit theorem, live

Why the normal is everywhere: average n draws from any light-tailed source — however skewed or lumpy — then standardize, and the distribution of those averages approaches N(0,1). Pick an ugly source, start at n = 1 to see its raw shape, then double n and watch the histogram get pulled into the bell.

Paper: §4.3 — PDF · Laplace (1812), de Moivre (1738)

Gamma — accumulated waiting

Shape k = 1 is the exponential; integer k is the wait for the k-th event; and as k grows the density symmetrizes toward a normal (the CLT, live). Scale θ stretches the axis. Gamma is the conjugate prior for Poisson and exponential rates — and the raw material for beta, chi-squared, t and F below.

It pays to read Gamma(k, θ) as the family's central interchange rather than one more curve: k counts how much has accumulated, θ sets the unit (mean kθ, variance kθ²). The special cases then stop being coincidences: the exponential is Gamma(1, 1/λ) — a single wait; Erlang is Gamma at integer k — the k-th Poisson arrival; χ²ₖ is Gamma(k/2, 2) — k half-waits, one per squared Gaussian dimension, so χ²₂ is Gamma(1, 2), an exponential in disguise (which is why a dart's squared miss distance is memoryless); Beta is the share G₁/(G₁+G₂) of one gamma in a pair; and as k → ∞ the accumulated sum turns normal — the CLT operating inside the family. One law, three hats: waiting time (queueing), accumulated evidence about a rate (the conjugate prior), and accumulated squared noise (χ²).

And the full family — fractional k and all — earns its keep in a place you can watch: gaps in traffic. Time the headways between passing cars. An empty road at night is memoryless (k = 1, the exponential); dense car-following traffic bunches near a safe gap (k large, the near-normal end); ordinary traffic sits in between and fits neither special case, so the standard headway model of traffic engineering is the full gamma (Pearson Type III) with fractional shape — 1/√k is a regularity dial you can watch rush hour turn. Meteorology learned the same lesson: the classic exponential raindrop-size spectrum (Marshall–Palmer 1948) over-counts both drizzle and the largest drops, and the accepted correction (Ulbrich 1983) is the gamma with its shape set free — once sampled by hand by letting drops fall into a pan of flour and sieving the pellets.

History: Euler's function (1729) became a statistical family in Karl Pearson's skew-curve system (1890s, Type III); the integer-shape case is Erlang's telephone-holding-time law (1909). Hydrology's standard rainfall model, insurance's aggregate loss, and — via Raiffa & Schlaifer's conjugate families (1961) — the Bayesian prior for every "how often?" question.

Paper: §4.4 & Figure 7 — PDF · Code: src/dist.c: ps_gamma() · Marsaglia & Tsang (2000)

Beta — the shape of an unknown proportion

Beta(a,b) lives on (0,1) and is the Bayesian's distribution for a probability. The buttons run the conjugate update that powers Bayesian A/B tests and Thompson-sampling bandits: observe a success and a gains one; observe a failure and b does. Better still, run the whole learning loop: set a hidden true p (the green line), press "observe 20 from true p" a few times, and watch the posterior find the truth and sharpen around it.

History: the oldest posterior in statistics — Bayes's 1763 essay computed exactly this update for an unknown chance (his billiard-table thought experiment), and Laplace's rule of succession (1774) integrated it. Thompson proposed sampling from it to allocate treatments in 1933; the idea slept for decades and woke up as the Thompson-sampling bandits inside modern ad and recommender systems.

Chi-squared — accumulated squared noise

Sum k squared standard normals and you get χ²k — which is exactly Gamma(k/2, 2). It is the law of the sample variance in a normal sample, the engine of goodness-of-fit tests, and the denominator of the t statistic.

The k = 2 case is the dartboard: aim at the bullseye with independent N(0, σ²) horizontal and vertical errors and the squared miss distance is σ²·χ²₂ — mean 2σ², variance 4σ⁴ (mean k, variance 2k in general), and exactly exponential. Rifle makers use it at the proof range: clamp the rifle in a machine rest so only its intrinsic dispersion remains, fire n rounds, then test the group centre against χ²₂ (is it zeroed, or systematically biased?) and the scatter about the centre against χ²2n−2 (is the dispersion within design tolerance σ₀?).

Its workhorse use is balls into bins: throw n balls (die rolls, PRNG outputs, hash keys) into m bins and compare observed counts Oi with expected Ei via Q = Σ(Oi−Ei)²/Ei ~ χ²m−1 (keep every Ei ≥ 5, merging sparse bins; lose one more df per fitted parameter). Under the null Q averages m−1 with variance 2(m−1), so Q/(m−1) should sit near 1: far above means counts too uneven (loaded die, biased generator, clustering hash), far below means too even — Fisher's complaint about Mendel. From Q come the p-value (one call to ps_chi_squared_cdf), the effect sizes φ² = Q/n and Cramér's V, and the equidistribution tests of the Diehard and NIST batteries covered by the sibling randtests survey.

In the field: the sibling experiment shastats (paper §4.6) ran one Pearson sum across 256 populations of 2²⁵ SHA-256 digests each, binned by leading-zero count (sparse bins pooled so every expected cell holds ≈32): χ² = 5139 on (256−1)(21−1) = 5100 df, p = 0.35 — a reduced statistic of 1.008 where the calibration says 1 ± 0.020. It also dodged a classic trap: a deterministic per-message weighting inflated its second histogram's total to 67 million, but only 22.4 million observations' worth of information — a design effect of 3 that every weighted χ² must be divided by, or everything reads "significant" from pure bookkeeping.

Like the binomial — a discrete count whose continuous limit is the normal — χ² works both sides of the discrete/continuous divide. Pearson's Q is built from integer counts and lives on a lattice; for m = 2 it is exactly the squared standardized binomial, so Q → χ²₁ is de Moivre–Laplace squared: χ² is to the multinomial what the normal is to the binomial. And the traffic flows back — every even-df tail is a finite Poisson sum, 1 − Fχ²2k(x) = Pr[Poisson(x/2) ≤ k−1], because χ²2k — the Gamma(k, 2) — is the waiting time to the k-th event of a Poisson process. The library uses the identity both ways: ps_poisson_cdf evaluates the discrete CDF with one call to the continuous incomplete gamma, and the driver watches discrete Q settle onto the continuous curve and prints even-df tails beside the Poisson sums they equal.

Try it — balls into bins, live. Each press of “throw” drops n balls uniformly into m bins, computes the discrete statistic Q = Σ(Oi−n/m)²/(n/m) for that one experiment (top panel), and adds it to the growing histogram of Q values (bottom panel). Watch the calibrations above come true: the integer-built Q fills in the smooth χ²m−1 curve, its running mean settles on m−1 and its variance on 2(m−1), and the share of throws past the 95% point settles on 5%. Then slide m down to 2 and n down to 100: the histogram turns into separated spikes — the discrete lattice showing through before the continuous limit takes over.

History: derived by the geodesist Helmert (1876) for surveying errors, then made universal by Pearson's goodness-of-fit criterion (1900) — among the most consequential papers in science. Fisher's 1922 degrees-of-freedom correction forged the modern df concept; Wilks (1938) put χ² under every likelihood-ratio test. In nature: a gas molecule's kinetic energy is a scaled χ²₃.

Student's t — heavy tails for small samples

Divide a normal by the square root of an independent normalized chi-squared and you get Student's t — a bell with heavier tails, because the scale had to be estimated. Slide ν and watch the tails fatten at small df and melt into the normal as ν grows. The readout shows how much more tail probability lies beyond ±1.96 than the normal's 5% — exactly why small samples demand bigger effects.

History: W. S. Gosset solved Guinness's small-sample barley problem in 1908 under the pseudonym "Student" (the brewery forbade publishing) — and checked his curve by shuffling cards through 750 samples of convict measurements, a Monte Carlo forty years early. Fisher's 1925 handbook and tables made it standard practice. As a scale mixture of normals it also models honest heavy-tailed data: lab runs of varying care, markets of varying calm.

Paper: §4.7 & Figure 11 — PDF · Code: src/dist.c: ps_student_t() · Student (1908)

Fisher's F — comparing variances

The ratio of two independent normalized chi-squareds. Since sample variances are scaled chi-squareds, F is the null distribution for ANOVA and regression F-tests. Two family facts close the loop: t²ν = F1,ν, and two-group ANOVA is the pooled t-test.

History: Fisher built the analysis of variance at Rothamsted's agricultural trials in the 1920s (randomize, replicate, block); he used z = ½ ln F, and Snedecor's 1934 tables named the plain ratio F in his honour. Every ANOVA in agronomy, psychology and industry — and every regression's overall F — descends from those wheat plots.

Rayleigh — the birthday bound

An ideal n-bit cryptographic hash is a random oracle: every input is a ball thrown uniformly into N = 2ⁿ bins. Throw until two balls share a bin and the waiting time, divided by √N, converges to a Rayleigh distribution — so collisions arrive after about 1.25·2^(n/2) evaluations. That is the birthday bound: the reason a 128-bit hash is collision-broken at 2⁶⁴ work, and the √N running time of Pollard's rho. Run the experiment below and watch the Rayleigh curve materialise out of raw collision counts — then flip on √N collapse, change the hash size, and keep running: trials from every N land on the same universal Rayleigh(1) curve, which is what "scaling limit" means.

History: Lord Rayleigh derived it in 1880 for the amplitude of many superposed sound waves of random phase — a 2-D random walk in disguise. The same geometry gives Rayleigh fading in wireless channels, radar clutter, and sea-wave heights.

Gumbel — the fullest bucket

The Rayleigh governs the first coincidence; the Gumbel governs the worst one. By the Fisher–Tippett theorem, the maximum of many light-tailed draws — minus its drift — converges to Gumbel: the CLT of maxima. For hashing it answers "how bad is the worst case?": fullest bucket, longest probe sequence, and the longest run of identical bits in a hash output stream (≈ log₂ n plus Gumbel fluctuation — the null of NIST's longest-run randomness test). The demo simulates the theorem exactly: maxima of n exponentials, minus ln n, against Gumbel(0,1).

History: born of the question "how large a flood must the dam survive?" — Fisher & Tippett proved the limit (1928), Gumbel's Statistics of Extremes (1958) made it engineering. BLAST E-values in bioinformatics are Gumbel tails, and ML's Gumbel-max trick turns softmax sampling into argmax of Gumbel noise.

In the field: the sibling experiment shastats (paper §4.10) kept each population's champion — the digest with the most leading zeros among 2²⁵ tries. Champions ranged from 22 to 33 zeros and matched the exact law of a geometric maximum, F(x)ⁿ (a lattice keeps its wobble, so the exact law, not the Gumbel limit), at p = 0.25. Extremes also priced its scariest avalanche cell: +3.9σ observed, but ≈4.35σ is expected for the largest of 6,400 two-sided readings — the worst number in the table was tamer than randomness predicts.
