2 · Where the randomness comes from

A pseudo-random generator (here xoshiro256++, Blackman & Vigna 2021) emits 64-bit integers; the top 53 bits become a uniform double U on [0,1). The master trick that turns U into anything else is inverse-transform sampling: push U through the inverse CDF, X = F−1(U). Watch it below: each uniform draw enters on the vertical axis, reflects off the CDF, and lands on the horizontal axis — where the curve is steep, the landings crowd together, and the histogram grows into an exponential density.

3 · The discrete family: counts

A yes/no event (Bernoulli), how many yeses in n tries (binomial — proportions, A/B tests, acceptance sampling), how long until the first yes (geometric — retries, attrition), and how many events occur when opportunities are many but each is rare (Poisson — arrivals, decays, claims, count regression). Drag the parameters and watch the shapes respond; hover any stem for its exact probability.

4 · The continuous family: measurements

Normal — the polar Box–Muller sampler

The normal's inverse CDF has no closed form, so the library earns its normals by rejection: rain uniform points on the square, keep the ~79% that land in the disc, and reshape each survivor's radius into a perfect bell curve (Box & Muller 1958). Press play and watch the histogram on the right converge to N(0,1).

Paper: §4.3 & Figure 4 — PDF · Code: src/dist.c: ps_normal() · Box & Muller (1958)

Gamma — accumulated waiting

Shape k = 1 is the exponential; integer k is the wait for the k-th event; and as k grows the density symmetrizes toward a normal (the CLT, live). Scale θ stretches the axis. Gamma is the conjugate prior for Poisson and exponential rates — and the raw material for beta, chi-squared, t and F below.

Paper: §4.4 & Figure 5 — PDF · Code: src/dist.c: ps_gamma() · Marsaglia & Tsang (2000)

Beta — the shape of an unknown proportion

Beta(a,b) lives on (0,1) and is the Bayesian's distribution for a probability. The buttons run the conjugate update that powers Bayesian A/B tests and Thompson-sampling bandits: observe a success and a gains one; observe a failure and b does. Watch certainty sharpen as evidence accumulates.

Paper: §4.5 & Figure 6 — PDF · Code: src/dist.c: ps_beta()

Chi-squared — accumulated squared noise

Sum k squared standard normals and you get χ²k — which is exactly Gamma(k/2, 2). It is the law of the sample variance in a normal sample, the engine of goodness-of-fit tests, and the denominator of the t statistic.

Paper: §4.6 — PDF · Code: src/dist.c: ps_chi_squared()

Student's t — heavy tails for small samples

Divide a normal by the square root of an independent normalized chi-squared and you get Student's t — a bell with heavier tails, because the scale had to be estimated. Slide ν and watch the tails fatten at small df and melt into the normal as ν grows. The readout shows how much more tail probability lies beyond ±1.96 than the normal's 5% — exactly why small samples demand bigger effects.

Paper: §4.7 & Figure 7 — PDF · Code: src/dist.c: ps_student_t() · Student (1908)

Fisher's F — comparing variances

The ratio of two independent normalized chi-squareds. Since sample variances are scaled chi-squareds, F is the null distribution for ANOVA and regression F-tests. Two family facts close the loop: t²ν = F1,ν, and two-group ANOVA is the pooled t-test.

Paper: §4.8 — PDF · Code: src/dist.c: ps_f()

5 · The payoff: a working t-test

The whole family funnels here. Model data as normal, estimate its mean and spread, and the standardized mean follows Student's t — so a tail area converts an observed sample into a p-value. This playground simulates a sample from N(10 + δ, σ) and tests H₀: mean = 10, exactly as ps_t_test_one_sample() does in C. Set δ = 0 to feel what a true null does; raise δ or n to watch power appear.

Press “draw sample & test”.

The p-value histogram fills in when you press “run 400 tests”: flat when H₀ is true (δ = 0), piled near zero when it is false.
